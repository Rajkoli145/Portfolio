### H1.1 — Transaction-Cost Collapse Dissolves the Persistent Firm Boundary

Coase's boundary condition (Section 2) states that a firm exists at whatever scale internal coordination is cheaper than market contracting. That condition was derived under an implicit assumption that search, negotiation, and monitoring costs are each non-trivial and roughly fixed per transaction. If agent-to-agent search cost approaches zero (agents can be discovered and matched instantly), negotiation cost approaches zero (terms can be settled by protocol rather than deliberation), and monitoring cost approaches zero (output is verifiable by cryptographic proof or automated check rather than human oversight), the Coasean condition for organizing *inside* a firm boundary should stop favoring persistent membership at almost any scale — the equilibrium organizational form should shift toward transient, per-task contracting rather than bounded, persistent firms.

If true, this directly challenges Chapter 1's definition requirement that an organization be "bounded" and "persistent" (Section 1) as a *necessary* feature rather than a feature that was only ever necessary because transaction costs were non-trivial.

**Proposed Experiment E1.1**
- *Research Question:* At what transaction-cost level does a simulated population of agents stop forming persistent bounded groups and instead settle into pure per-task spot contracting?
- *Method:* Agent-based market simulation where agents can either (a) join a persistent organization with fixed membership and internal task allocation, or (b) contract per-task on an open market, with configurable search/negotiation/monitoring cost parameters approaching zero across simulation runs.
- *Metrics:* Equilibrium organizational persistence (mean membership duration), boundary stability (rate of membership churn), aggregate task throughput under each regime.
- *Expected Results:* A negative relationship between transaction-cost parameters and organizational persistence, with persistent firms surviving only where a *non*-transaction-cost justification (e.g., liability concentration, capital pooling, or regulatory requirement) is separately modeled in.
- *Limitations:* Real transaction costs may never approach the simulation's limiting case; the model may need to separately hold constant a liability/accountability requirement to test whether that alone sustains the firm boundary independent of transaction cost.

### H1.2 — Verification Displaces Reputation as the Basis of Trust

Chapter 1's Section 8 states "trust substitutes for costly verification" as a universal principle. This framing implies trust and verification are substitutes on a single axis — as verification cost falls, less trust should be required to sustain cooperation. Human organizational trust, however, is built substantially through repeated interaction and reputation (in the Axelrod sense) precisely because per-instance verification has historically been expensive. For AI agents, if per-action verification (cryptographic attestation, formal proof of computation, deterministic replay) becomes cheap, reputation-based trust mechanisms should become largely unnecessary rather than simply supplemented — a discontinuous shift, not merely a marginal one.

**Proposed Experiment E1.2**
- *Research Question:* Does per-action verifiability substitute for repeated-interaction-based reputation in sustaining cooperative equilibria among agents?
- *Method:* Iterated multi-agent cooperation simulation (e.g., a repeated Prisoner's Dilemma-style task-delegation game) run in two regimes: (a) reputation-only, no per-action verification, (b) cheap per-action verification, no reputation history retained. Introduce adversarial defecting agents in both regimes.
- *Metrics:* Rate of successful cooperation, cost of detecting a defector, system resilience to a new (reputation-less) adversarial entrant.
- *Expected Results:* The verification-only regime should detect and exclude defectors faster and should not require the "cold start" period reputation systems impose on new entrants, at the cost of requiring a verification infrastructure that reputation systems do not.
- *Limitations:* Assumes verification is unforgeable; does not model cases where the *quality* of a decision, rather than its factual execution, is what needs verifying (see H1.5-adjacent concern in `notes.md`).

### H1.3 — The Substrate-Independent Analog to Dunbar's Number Is a Bandwidth/Latency Ratio, Not a Fixed Headcount

Section 3.1 grounds hunter-gatherer band size in Dunbar's number — a cognitive ceiling on stable relationships, generally attributed to human neocortical capacity. Chapter 1's Section 9 correctly notes this ceiling is human-specific. But the chapter does not propose what, if anything, replaces it. A plausible substrate-independent analog: any flat (non-hierarchical) coordination scheme has an effective span-of-coordination determined by the ratio of available messaging bandwidth/consensus latency to the per-pair coordination overhead the task requires — not by any fixed number of trackable relationships. This predicts AI-native flat organizations can be substantially larger than 150 nodes before coordination degrades, but that they still degrade, and that the degradation curve's *shape* should resemble the shape of human coordination-overhead curves (governed by the same underlying combinatorial pairwise-interaction growth), even though the *location* of the breakdown point shifts by orders of magnitude.

**Proposed Experiment E1.3**
- *Research Question:* Does flat multi-agent coordination degrade according to the same functional form as human Dunbar-number degradation, merely shifted by a bandwidth/latency scaling factor?
- *Method:* Vary agent population size in a flat (no hierarchy) peer-coordination task at several fixed messaging-bandwidth/latency levels; separately, compare against published human small-group coordination degradation data at equivalent group sizes.
- *Metrics:* Coordination overhead per additional agent, task throughput vs. group size, point of onset of throughput decline.
- *Expected Results:* Onset-of-decline point shifts rightward with bandwidth, but the shape of the decline curve (e.g., whether it is linear, quadratic, or exponential in overhead) should match across human and simulated-agent data if the underlying driver is combinatorial pairwise interaction rather than anything specific to human cognition.
- *Limitations:* Comparable, well-controlled human small-group datasets at large N are scarce; most Dunbar's-number evidence is observational and social-network-derived rather than experimental.

### H1.4 — Mission Command's Benefit Persists Under Zero Latency Because It Solves an Information Problem, Not a Communication-Speed Problem

Chapter 1's Section 7 presents military mission command (*auftragstaktik*) as a case of decentralized execution compensating for unreliable, slow communication with distant units. A natural reading is that mission command becomes unnecessary once communication is instantaneous. An alternative, competing interpretation — consistent with the Hayekian knowledge-problem justification in Section 2 — is that mission command's advantage was never really about latency at all, but about placing decision authority where the relevant *local information* already sits, which remains true even at zero latency, because relaying raw information to a central decision-maker is not the same as relaying the tacit, context-dependent judgment needed to interpret it.

**Proposed Experiment E1.4**
- *Research Question:* Does the performance advantage of decentralized (mission-command-style) decision authority over centralized decision authority vanish as communication latency approaches zero, or does it persist due to information asymmetry independent of latency?
- *Method:* Simulation of a task environment with genuine local information asymmetry (some state variables observable only to specific agents), comparing centralized-authority and decentralized-authority decision schemes across a range of communication latencies from high to near-zero.
- *Metrics:* Decision quality (task outcome vs. an omniscient-benchmark decision), decision latency, degradation of centralized-authority performance as information asymmetry increases at fixed (near-zero) communication latency.
- *Expected Results:* Centralized authority should converge toward decentralized authority's performance as communication latency falls — but only if information asymmetry is also low; where local information asymmetry remains high, decentralized authority should retain an advantage regardless of communication speed.
- *Limitations:* Requires careful separation of "raw data transfer" from "judgment transfer" in the simulation, which is a modeling choice that could be contested; real information asymmetry is often about which questions to even ask, not just which facts are known.

### H1.5 — Correlated Replication Undermines, Rather Than Strengthens, the Risk-Pooling Justification for AI Organizations

Chapter 1's Section 2 lists risk pooling — absorbing the failure of any single member, smoothing variance across many independent efforts — as one of four reasons organizations exist. This justification's statistical force depends on member failures being substantially *independent*. Human organizations approximate this because individual humans differ in training, judgment, and error patterns. An AI organization composed of many instances of the same or closely related models may instead exhibit *correlated* failure: the same systematic blind spot, bias, or training-data gap appearing simultaneously across many "members," at precisely the moment risk pooling is most needed (e.g., all instances failing on the same edge case at once). If so, risk pooling is not a justification that simply "carries over" for AI organizations (as a superficial reading of Section 9's substrate-independence framing might suggest) — it may actively work in reverse unless deliberate diversity is engineered in.

**Proposed Experiment E1.5**
- *Research Question:* Does an organization composed of many instances of a similar underlying model exhibit lower effective risk-pooling benefit than an organization of equivalent size with independently-varying members?
- *Method:* Simulate a risk-pooling task (e.g., independent error-correction across parallel task attempts) using (a) agents with deliberately correlated error distributions (shared model/shared blind spot) and (b) agents with independently-drawn error distributions, at matched aggregate error rates.
- *Metrics:* Variance reduction achieved by pooling, worst-case simultaneous-failure rate, effective "independent equivalent" population size.
- *Expected Results:* Correlated-error agents should show markedly lower variance reduction from pooling and a non-trivial probability of simultaneous correlated failure that independent-error agents would not exhibit at the same nominal group size.
- *Limitations:* Real correlation structure among deployed AI agents (shared base model, shared fine-tuning, shared prompt) is difficult to estimate without access to production failure data; the simulation necessarily stipulates a correlation structure rather than measuring one.

### H1.6 — Cheap Exit Weakens Voice as a Governance Mechanism in AI Organizations

Chapter 1's Section 8 lists "exit and voice" (Hirschman) as a universal accountability lever, illustrated by Linux's fork rights in Section 7. For humans, exit is costly (career risk, social disruption, loss of accumulated relationship capital), which is part of why voice — working to change the organization from within — is frequently exercised first. For AI agents or agent populations that can be forked or replicated at near-zero cost, exit costs approach zero as well. This predicts that dissatisfied sub-populations of an AI organization should default to forking rather than exercising voice far more readily than human members do, which would make AI-organization governance more prone to fragmentation and less prone to internal consensus-seeking than the Linux/Wikipedia case studies in Section 7 might suggest by analogy.

**Proposed Experiment E1.6**
- *Research Question:* Does reducing the cost of exit (forking) shift a simulated governance dispute toward fragmentation and away from voice-based resolution, relative to a matched scenario with costly exit?
- *Method:* Simulate a governance disagreement within an agent organization (e.g., disagreement over a shared resource-allocation rule) under varying fork/exit costs, measuring whether the dispute resolves via negotiated rule change (voice) or via population split (exit).
- *Metrics:* Rate of fork vs. negotiated resolution, post-resolution population fragmentation, task-coherence loss following fragmentation.
- *Expected Results:* Lower exit cost should correlate with a higher fork rate and a lower rate of negotiated internal resolution, converging toward near-total reliance on exit as exit cost approaches zero.
- *Limitations:* Real forking has downstream coordination costs (duplicated infrastructure, split user/resource bases) that are not fully captured by a simplified "exit cost" parameter, and which may reintroduce some of voice's advantage indirectly.
