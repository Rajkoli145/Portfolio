### Gaps identified in the chapter's current treatment

The chapter leans heavily on Coase (transaction costs) and Hayek (the knowledge problem) as its economic foundation, and on Hirschman for exit/voice, but does not go further into the agency-theory and asset-specificity literature that would deepen Section 2's transaction-cost argument, nor into the non-hierarchical commons-governance literature that would help close the case-study gap flagged in `notes.md`.

### Candidate additions for future consideration

- **Michael Jensen & William Meckling, "Theory of the Firm: Managerial Behavior, Agency Costs, and Ownership Structure," *Journal of Financial Economics* (1976).** The foundational treatment of principal-agent problems and agency costs — directly relevant to any future chapter on incentive and mechanism design (per Chapter 0's roadmap), and a natural complement to Section 2's transaction-cost argument, which currently stops at Coase without addressing what happens once an agent's interests diverge from its principal's.
- **Oliver Williamson, *Markets and Hierarchies: Analysis and Antitrust Implications* (1975).** Extends Coase with the concept of asset specificity — why some transactions are more prone to opportunism outside a firm boundary than others. Relevant to H1.1 in `hypotheses.md`: if transaction costs collapse toward zero, asset specificity (rather than search/negotiation/monitoring cost) may become the residual reason a firm boundary persists at all.
- **Robert Axelrod, *The Evolution of Cooperation* (1984).** The canonical account of how reputation and repeated interaction sustain cooperation under uncertainty — the mechanism Section 8's "trust substitutes for costly verification" principle implicitly assumes. Needed before H1.2 can be properly framed as a genuine substitution rather than an assumed one.
- **Albert O. Hirschman, *Exit, Voice, and Loyalty: Responses to Decline in Firms, Organizations, and States* (1970).** Referenced by name in Section 8 and illustrated via the Linux case study in Section 7, but not yet given a full citation in the chapter's own reading list. Should be added directly rather than left as an implicit reference.
- **James G. March & Herbert A. Simon, *Organizations* (1958).** A deeper and more structural treatment of bounded rationality and organizational decision-making than Simon's *Administrative Behavior* (already listed in Chapter 0's bibliography); would strengthen Section 8's bounded-rationality principle with more explicit mechanism.
- **Elinor Ostrom, *Governing the Commons: The Evolution of Institutions for Collective Action* (1990).** Directly addresses the case-study gap noted in `notes.md`: coordination institutions that emerge and persist without central design or market pricing, governing shared resources through locally-evolved rules. The strongest available candidate for closing both the case-study gap (Section 7) and the non-Western/non-corporate sourcing gap carried forward from Chapter 0's bibliography notes, since Ostrom's fieldwork spans irrigation communities, fisheries, and forest-management institutions across multiple non-Western contexts.
- **Yochai Benkler, "Coase's Penguin, or, Linux and The Nature of the Firm," *Yale Law Journal* (2002).** Extends Coase's own framework directly to the Linux case study already used in Section 7, asking why some production is organized as neither firm nor market but as "commons-based peer production." Would let the chapter's existing Linux case study be analyzed through a framework built specifically for it, rather than only through the general transaction-cost lens borrowed from Section 2.

### Explicitly flagged as unresolved

The non-Western organizational-tradition gap flagged in Chapter 0's reading notes (`00-vision/reading.md`) has not been closed by this chapter's bibliography or case studies either — if anything, Section 7's case-study selection makes the gap more visible, since it is drawn entirely from Western corporate, military, and open-source institutions. Ostrom's fieldwork (above) is the strongest candidate identified so far for narrowing this gap, but a systematic search for non-Western coordination and governance traditions — beyond a single author's fieldwork — remains an open item rather than a closed one.

### Update — web search pass (2026-07-26)

A live search pass turned up two concrete, checkable candidate traditions worth weighing against the current case-study sample rather than leaving the gap purely theoretical. Both are flagged here as candidates only — neither has been read in full yet, so neither should be cited in the published chapter until that happens.

- **Tyson Yunkaporta, *Sand Talk: How Indigenous Thinking Can Save the World* (2019), and Yunkaporta et al., "(Non-)Human Coordination Dynamics: Scaling Transformative Processes Through Indigenous Knowledge Systems," *Complexity* (2026).** The 2026 paper describes a systems-change network built on Indigenous relational processes, arguing that the coordination pattern of traditional Australasian foraging groups scales outward as a holarchy of interdependent relations, extending decentralized social organization from local to distant contexts, and explicitly engaging team-scaling limits, the cognitive limits of social relations, and network effects. That last point makes it a strong candidate specifically for H1.3 in `hypotheses.md` (the bandwidth/latency analog to Dunbar's number), not just for the general case-study gap. This is a very recent (2026) paper with essentially no independent citation history yet — it should be read in full and weighed against that fact before being relied on for a strong claim in the published chapter.
- **Ubuntu-based African organizational and leadership theory** — candidate sources include Luchien Karsten & Honorine Illa, "Ubuntu as a Key African Management Concept," *Journal of Managerial Psychology* (2005), and Johann Broodryk, *Ubuntu Management Philosophy* (2005). This tradition frames organizational decision-making as inherently communal and restorative rather than individualistic, replacing adversarial or purely hierarchical resolution with consensus-seeking that centers relationships as the substance of the work rather than a background condition for it. This is a candidate for the incentive/governance chapters as much as for Chapter 1 — it bears on Section 8's authority and incentive principles, not only on the historical-forms material in Section 3.

Neither of these fully resolves the gap on its own — one is a single, very recent research program (Yunkaporta), the other a body of applied-management literature still actively working out its own relationship to Western institutional theory. Both should be read in full before Chapter 2 leans on "cross-disciplinary convergence" as evidence in a way that implicitly generalizes from Western-only cases.

### Update — full-text read-through (2026-07-26)

Following through on the candidates above rather than leaving them as search snippets:

**Yunkaporta et al. (2026) — attempted, partially successful.** The publisher page (Wiley) blocked direct fetching. A second search pass did surface a substantially fuller abstract than the first: the paper's central construct is "coordination dynamics" defined not as individual neurology or synchronized response to shared external stimuli, but as a kinship structure that makes behavioral protocols interoperable across large, non-co-located populations — termed "one belly" in the paper's own vocabulary, framed as a form of distributed cognition the authors attribute directly to land and inherited relational structure. This is worth flagging precisely: the paper's claims operate in two registers at once — an empirical/structural claim about coordination scaling that is directly relevant to H1.3, and an ontological claim (that land itself performs a cognitive "thinking-feeling" function) that is a metaphysical commitment, not a testable structural claim in the sense this handbook otherwise requires. A future chapter drawing on this source should engage the structural claim on its own terms and be explicit that it is doing so without adjudicating the ontological claim, rather than importing both uncritically or dismissing both because of the second. Full paper text was still not read; this remains a partial verification.

**David W. Lutz, "African Ubuntu Philosophy and Global Management" — read in full.** This substantially upgrades what was previously a search-snippet-level candidate. Two things worth recording:

1. The paper is explicitly a normative philosophical argument, not an empirical organizational-science study — it argues what a management theory grounded in communal (rather than individualist) human nature *should* look like, drawing on virtue ethics, not a study of how existing organizations actually coordinate. This matters for how the handbook uses it: it is a source for Chapter 2 onward's treatment of incentive design and the philosophical assumptions behind "the firm as a nexus of individual contracts" (a framing the paper explicitly contests, citing Jensen & Meckling 1976 directly), not a substitute case study of the kind Section 7 needs.
2. The paper is a much richer source than initially indicated: it explicitly triangulates *three* non-Western/non-modern traditions against each other — Ubuntu, Confucian business ethics, and the pre-Enlightenment Aristotelian tradition — and treats the Anglo-American shareholder-value and stakeholder theories as the outlier requiring justification, not the default. This reframing is itself relevant to Q0.2 in the Chapter 0 questions file (whether cross-disciplinary convergence is genuine evidence or an artifact of shared tradition): this paper's argument is precisely that convergence across Ubuntu, Confucian, and classical Western virtue ethics is *not* an artifact of shared tradition, since they developed independently, which is a data point worth weighing when that methodological question is eventually addressed head-on.

New citable sources surfaced by reading this paper in full (not previously identified):
- Kwame Gyekye, *The Unexamined Life: Philosophy and the African Experience* (Sankofa, 1996).
- Thaddeus Metz, "Toward an African Moral Theory," *Journal of Political Philosophy*, Vol. 15, No. 3 (2007), pp. 331–334 — the paper's own account credits Metz with bringing philosophical precision to competing interpretations of ubuntu, distinguishing a self-realization account from a harmony/community account, and arguing for the latter.
- Kam-hon Lee, "Moral Consideration and Strategic Management Moves: The Chinese Case," *Management Decision*, Vol. 34, No. 9 (1996) — a specifically Confucian-business-ethics source, useful if a future chapter wants an East Asian tradition distinct from Ubuntu rather than folding both into one "non-Western" category.
- Lovemore Mbigi, *Ubuntu: The Spirit of African Transformation Management* (Knowres, 2005), and Johann Broodryk, *Ubuntu Management Philosophy* (Knowres, 2005) — both already flagged as candidates above, now confirmed as directly quoted primary sources in a peer-reviewable secondary text rather than only search-result mentions.

This is now a genuinely usable source rather than a lead. It should not, however, be substituted for a direct read of Karsten & Illa (2005) or Broodryk (2005) themselves if either is quoted or relied on specifically in a future chapter — Lutz's paper is a synthesis and argument built partly on those sources, not a replacement for reading them directly.

### Update — closing out remaining leads (2026-07-26, continued)

**Elinor Ostrom, *Governing the Commons* (1990) — substantively engaged, not just cited.** This closes the case-study gap identified in `notes.md` ("On the case study sample") more directly than any other source found so far, because Ostrom's entire project was built by studying commons institutions that were *not* deliberately engineered by an identifiable founder, in direct contrast to all six of Chapter 1's current Section 7 case studies. Her argument was built against Garrett Hardin's "tragedy of the commons," using long-lived, self-governing common-pool-resource institutions as counter-evidence — among them a Swiss alpine village (Törbel) whose commoning practices date to the 15th century, Philippine irrigation communities, and Japanese village commons. From this comparative case analysis she derived eight design principles distinguishing durable self-governing institutions from failed ones: clearly defined boundaries; rules for resource use matched to local conditions; collective-choice arrangements letting those affected by rules change them; monitoring by or accountable to the community itself; graduated sanctions rather than binary punishment; low-cost, accessible conflict-resolution mechanisms; the community's recognized right to devise its own rules without an external authority overriding them; and, for larger commons, nested layers of governance rather than a single flat rule-set. This is directly citable and directly usable for a future governance chapter, not only as a non-Western-adjacent case study but as a source of alternative design principles to compare against Chapter 1's own Section 8 universal-principles list (worth testing whether Ostrom's eight collapse into, extend, or contradict the seven listed there).

**Luchien Karsten & Honorine Illa, "Ubuntu as a Key African Management Concept" (2005) — citation confirmed, full text paywalled.** Confirmed exact citation: *Journal of Managerial Psychology*, Vol. 20, No. 7, pp. 607–620. Worth flagging precisely what the abstract itself claims, since it is more modest than how the source is sometimes invoked secondhand (including, arguably, in Lutz's paper above): the paper's own stated finding is that Ubuntu's applicability in companies depends on the individual manager's *habitus* as a good conversationalist — a specific, narrower claim about interpersonal practice, not a general claim that Ubuntu functions as a full alternative organizational design framework. A future chapter citing this source should cite that specific, bounded finding rather than the broader Ubuntu-management narrative built around it elsewhere.

**Kam-hon Lee, "Moral Consideration and Strategic Management Moves: The Chinese Case" (1996) — citation confirmed, full text paywalled.** Confirmed exact citation: *Management Decision*, Vol. 34, No. 9, pp. 65–70. The abstract confirms Lutz's characterization: the paper argues moral consideration (distinct in form from Western business ethics) is a genuine factor in Chinese strategic business decisions, drawn from Chinese business history and the practice of overseas Chinese business communities. A stronger, more recent, and more directly theory-building companion source was found in the process of checking this one: Charlene Tan, "Integrating Moral Personhood and Moral Management: A Confucian Approach to Ethical Leadership," *Journal of Business Ethics*, Vol. 191, No. 1 (2024), pp. 167–177 — this draws on four Confucian classical texts directly rather than a single strategic-management case study, and argues moral personhood and moral management are inseparable in a Confucian leadership framework. This is a stronger primary candidate than Lee (1996) if a future chapter wants one clearly-argued Confucian leadership source rather than a 1996 trade-management-journal piece.
