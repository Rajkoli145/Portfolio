### On tooling vs. organization (re: Section 0.2, "From Studying Systems to Studying Organizations")

The neuron/sociology analogy used in the chapter is useful but has a limit worth flagging: unlike a neuron, an individual tool-using agent is already organization-like in miniature — it internally sequences sub-goals, allocates attention across competing signals, and arbitrates conflicting objectives, all of which are organizational problems recursively nested inside what looks like a single unit.

This suggests the neuron/organization boundary drawn for convenience in Chapter 0 may not be a clean level-of-description split at all. A sufficiently complex individual agent may already exhibit some of the coordination failures normally attributed to multi-agent systems, internally, before any second agent is added. Not resolved here — it argues for caution before assuming the boundary the chapter draws is as clean as it's presented, and it's the source of H0.1's framing (see `hypotheses.md`).

### On interdisciplinary triangulation (re: Section 0.3, "An Interdisciplinary Foundation")

The list of eight disciplines in the chapter is deliberately broad, which creates its own risk: it becomes easy to invoke whichever field's framing is convenient for a given argument while quietly ignoring that field's own internal disputes.

Cybernetics, for instance, is presented through Ashby and Wiener's classical, control-theoretic lens. But second-order cybernetics (associated with Heinz von Foerster) explicitly questions whether an observer can ever stand fully outside the system it is trying to regulate — which bears directly on whether a governance layer overseeing an autonomous organization can be a genuinely neutral external verifier, or is always partially entangled with what it audits. This is a live tension the chapter doesn't surface, since it would have pulled the prose into a methodological aside rather than the framing argument the section is making.

*Update (2026-07-26):* the canonical citation for this point, previously unidentified, has now been confirmed via a live search — see the "Update — web search verification pass" section in `reading.md`. Von Foerster's own primary source is "Cybernetics of Cybernetics" (1974/1979), collected in *Understanding Understanding: Essays on Cybernetics and Cognition* (2003). This tension can now be cited properly if a future chapter takes it up rather than left as an unsupported allusion.

A systematic check for cross-disciplinary convergence should also draw on organizational and coordination traditions outside the primarily Western canon the current reading list pulls from — see `reading.md` for specifics.

### On research principles and falsifiability (re: Section 0.5, "Approach and Method")

The four commitments stated in Section 0.5 are sound methodological stances but are not yet operationalized as written — nothing in the chapter specifies how a reader would actually detect if the handbook had violated them.

Proposed operationalization (not adopted in the chapter, kept here as a candidate for future editorial process): tag every mechanism the handbook discusses with (a) the problem it solves, (b) the participant assumption it depends on, and (c) whether that assumption is Human-Specific, Substrate-Independent, or Undetermined. Chapter 1's discussion of which assumptions transfer from human to autonomous organizations already applies this discipline informally; the proposal is to make it a standing template for every future chapter, so a reader could audit whether a mechanism labeled "Substrate-Independent" in one chapter quietly contradicts one labeled "Human-Specific" for the same underlying phenomenon elsewhere. This is an editorial-process idea, not a research hypothesis, so it sits here rather than in `hypotheses.md`.
